A blog post by Terence Eden, citing an Interisle report, reveals that in 2025, 85 million new gTLD domains were registered, and by May 2026, 8.5 million (10%) had been blocklisted for malicious activity, with the actual abuse rate likely closer to 20%. This highlights a critical flaw in internet governance, as DNS, a fundamental infrastructure, is being exploited at an alarming rate for scams. It underscores the need for stronger registration policies and better enforcement by ICANN and registries to protect users. The Interisle report analyzed cybercrime supply chains and found that TLDs with stronger identity verification requirements have lower abuse rates. ICANN's current requirements are minimal, and the issue has been discussed for years without effective action.

rss · Simon Willison · Sep 6, 14:40

**Background**: The Domain Name System (DNS) translates human-readable domain names into IP addresses, and is governed by ICANN, which oversees top-level domains (TLDs) like .com and .org. Cybercriminals often register new domains for phishing, malware, and spam, and blocklists are used to identify malicious domains. The high abuse rate among new gTLDs indicates that current registration and verification processes are insufficient to deter criminal activity.

He recommends incremental refactoring with automated tests instead of greenfield replacements. This insight is significant for software engineering teams facing technical debt, as it challenges the common 'rewrite from scratch' approach. It highlights the risks of parallel systems and the importance of incremental modernization, which can save time and resources. Willison notes that while the old system remains in production, it continues to change, and its developers lack motivation to maintain it well, increasing debt. The new team often underestimates the complexity, leading to a partial launch and two systems in production, with the risk of abandonment.

**Background**: Technical debt refers to the implied cost of additional rework caused by choosing an easy solution now instead of a better approach that would take longer. The 'rewrite vs. incremental' debate is a classic in software engineering, where rewrites often fail due to the moving target problem and lack of understanding of the existing system's behavior.

A Reddit discussion argues that reproducibility in machine learning research is becoming unattainable due to the rise of physical AI requiring expensive hardware, unreliable demos, and unverifiable claims from large AI companies. The post questions whether reproducibility should be abandoned and seeks community input on future implementation. This discussion highlights a growing crisis in ML research credibility, affecting researchers, practitioners, and policymakers who rely on published results. If reproducibility continues to decline, it could undermine scientific progress and lead to misguided decisions based on unverified claims. The author cites three main reasons: physical AI experiments require expensive labs and high-speed cameras, demos are selectively shown and may fail after recording, and big companies make vague claims with financial incentives to exaggerate. They contrast this with historical projects like the atomic bomb, which had high internal reproducibility and mathematical rigor.

**Background**: Reproducibility is a core principle of scientific research, allowing others to verify and build upon findings. In ML, this often requires sharing code, data, and detailed experimental setups. However, recent trends such as proprietary models, expensive hardware, and subjective benchmarks have made independent verification increasingly difficult. The discussion reflects broader concerns in the AI community about transparency and accountability.
